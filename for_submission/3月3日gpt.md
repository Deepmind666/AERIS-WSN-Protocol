Fatal reference integrity problems

P0 (Fatal): Reference 16 is misattributed. The DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-01841-2 resolves to a Scientific Reports article authored by G. Pushpa et al. with article identifier 15:16681; the manuscript cites different authors (“Zhang, L.; Wang, C.; Liu, J.”) and a different article number.

P0 (Fatal): Reference 17 is misattributed. The DOI 10.1038/s41598-024-75904-1 Crossmark metadata lists authors El‑Sayed, Abd‑Elgaber, Zanaty, Alsubaei, Almazroi, Bakheet; the manuscript cites “Kumar, A.; Sharma, P.”.

These are not mere formatting mistakes: they indicate the bibliography was not validated against DOIs and undermines trust in the remainder of the manuscript’s claim-to-evidence traceability.
Claim support spot-check of key citations

Below are 10+ “load-bearing” claims that I spot-checked against the cited sources.

P1 (Major): The manuscript’s “channel realism” motivation is supported by classic low-power link unreliability/asymmetry literature, but it does not follow through by specifying its own channel model parameters. Zúñiga & Krishnamachari’s transitional-region work explicitly supports that packet reception rate varies with distance and depends on channel parameters including path loss exponent and shadowing variance. Yet AERIS only names four regimes and “strict-physics flags” without giving the actual propagation/interference parameters needed for reproduction.

P2 (Minor): LEACH/PEGASIS/HEED/TEEN baseline characterizations are directionally correct. HEED is indeed a distributed clustering protocol balancing energy and communication costs. TEEN is indeed a threshold-sensitive (“reactive”) reporting approach. PEGASIS forms a chain for data gathering and designates a leader to transmit to the base station.

P2 (Minor): Link-quality estimation is correctly cited as important to WSN performance. The ACM TOSN survey on link-quality estimation describes the importance of LQE for higher-layer protocol performance.

P2 (Minor): “Learning-based routing incurs training/complexity overhead” is supported by the cited RL routing papers. “MeFi” is explicitly a mean-field reinforcement-learning routing approach (implying training and policy learning). The tactical packet routing work is explicitly multi-agent DRL in a specialized setting (again implying training and model complexity).

P1 (Major): Several “recent work” citations are thematically adjacent but not actually routing or not aligned with the claim they are used to support. Example: the IEEE TII reference resolves to an NLOS localization paper, not routing; if it is supporting “environment uncertainty modeling,” that needs to be stated explicitly, otherwise it looks like padding.
Missing 2022–2026 references that should be cited

P1 (Major): The manuscript is positioned as “IoT sensors” routing but restricts evaluation to LEACH/PEGASIS/HEED/TEEN; it omits (or at least does not cite) key modern families of IoT/LLN routing and recent “adaptive switching” work. At minimum, it should acknowledge and cite recent “adaptive switching” protocol design papers, because your novelty pitch centers on CAS (Context-Adaptive Switching). One directly on-point recent paper explicitly titled around “Adaptive Switching and Routing Protocol…” exists (ScienceDirect/Elsevier).

P1 (Major): Gateway-assisted hierarchical routing is not novel per se; recent gateway-based protocols exist and should be cited when claiming novelty for “Gateway reinforcement.” A recent PLOS ONE paper describes an “energy efficient hierarchical gateway routing protocol” (EHGR). Even if AERIS differs, the related-work section must position the contribution relative to such gateway-routing concepts.

P2 (Minor): If positioning AERIS as “learning-light” and practical, cite recent RL-based energy-balanced routing as contrast. Example: an RL-based energy-balanced routing approach in Soft Computing (published 2023) exists and explicitly frames RL overhead/efficiency as a challenge.
Phase 2 Statistical Rigor Audit

The manuscript explicitly claims Welch t-tests, Holm correction, and Hedges’ g effect sizes. That is directionally better than the typical Sensors submission in this area, but there are serious methodological/reproducibility gaps.
Sample sizes and independence

P1 (Major): n=30 for the “comparability” block is plausible, but the paper does not justify independence or the randomization unit; n=3200 per cell is so large that pseudo-replication becomes a real risk. The experimental setup states “30 independent seeds” for multi-environment/ablation and “n=3200 per environment-node-protocol cell” for scalability. If those 3200 are not truly independent trial seeds (e.g., if they are per-round samples within the same topology and then treated as independent), the p-values and CIs are invalid.

P1 (Major): With n=3200 and n=1000 cells, statistical significance becomes almost guaranteed; practical significance must be the primary lens. You partly recognize this by using ΔPDR and effect size maps, but you still repeatedly report Holm-corrected p-values as if they are evidentiary rather than inevitable.
Multiple comparisons and Holm–Bonferroni

P1 (Major): “Holm correction within each reported comparison family” is underspecified; readers cannot verify the family definition or the multiplicity count. The paper states Holm correction is used “within each reported comparison family,” but does not define the family boundaries or m for each table/figure in a reproducible way. The Holm procedure is well-defined (step-down sequential rejective Bonferroni). Your paper needs to state the exact hypothesis set per family (e.g., “all protocol pairs within each environment×node count” or “AERIS vs strongest baseline per cell”) and the exact count.
Effect sizes and interpretation (Hedges’ g)

P1 (Major): Using Welch’s t-test (unequal variances) but reporting a pooled-SD standardized mean difference without discussing variance heterogeneity is at minimum incomplete. The manuscript defines Hedges’ g with a pooled SD (sp) and a small-sample correction J. NIST’s reference documentation distinguishes pooled-SD variants (Hedges’ g vs Cohen’s d variants). If you are invoking Welch’s test because variances differ, you need to justify why a pooled-SD standardizer is still appropriate, or use/report an unequal-variance effect size alongside it.

P2 (Minor): Using Cohen-like “small/medium/large” thresholds for g is not inherently wrong, but must be contextualized. Cohen’s thresholds are heuristics, not standards, and were never tailored to simulation-derived PDR metrics. The manuscript partially acknowledges instability of standardized effects at large n (e.g., your note that standardized effects can become “extremely large”). That caveat should be elevated from footnote-level to the main statistical interpretation logic.
PDR metric definition and risk of inflated reliability claims

P1 (Major): The PDR definition is non-standardly named and may bias comparisons unless “source_packets_expected” is rigorously defined. The paper defines
PDRexpected = bs_delivered / source_packets_expected, and says all conclusions are based on it.
Standard PDR is typically defined as delivered packets divided by original sent packets. Without a precise definition of “expected” (e.g., “generated at the application layer given node alive status and traffic model”) and without confirming it’s identical across protocols (especially TEEN, which is inherently threshold/event-driven), “expected” risks embedding a protocol-dependent denominator and distorting comparisons.
Diagnostic/pilot tables and disclosure boundaries

P2 (Minor, done correctly): The “rigor-patch pilot” is explicitly labeled as diagnostic and “not used in primary claim tables.” This is good practice.

P2 (Minor): Stress-delta language is clear, but the “patch minus control” design still needs a fully specified configuration diff. The manuscript defines stress-delta comparisons as patch-minus-control under matched environment/node settings. You do not provide a complete, reproducible enumeration of what “strict physics” switches on/off, which is necessary for interpreting stress-delta numerically.
Uncertainty reporting (SDs/CIs)

P1 (Major): CIs/SDs are not consistently provided for the quantitative claims that drive conclusions. You state “two-sided 95% intervals” and show CI bands in at least one figure caption, but many tables present mean deltas and corrected p-values without CIs. At minimum, tables that are used for claim support must include dispersion (SD/SE) and/or confidence intervals.
Phase 3 Claim Boundary Verification

The paper makes a serious attempt to fence claims by evidence regime, but the fencing is not airtight.

P2 (Minor, good practice): Table 2 (evidence-regime map) is an explicit attempt to prevent scope creep.

P2 (Minor, mostly compliant): The abstract explicitly acknowledges the key counterexample regime: PEGASIS dominates indoor_office at high scale. This is the sort of qualification most WSN routing papers omit.

P2 (Minor, good practice): NS-3 is explicitly limited to “trend-level” validation and you do not claim cross-platform numeric equivalence.

P1 (Major): The manuscript still blurs the boundary between “trend evidence” and “quantitative superiority evidence” by reporting many corrected p-values and “rank-1” language without always tying it to a specific regime. “Rank-1 in three environments” is only defensible for the large-scale regime as defined (100–1000 nodes with the stated strict-physics configuration). That scope should be repeated wherever “better than X” language appears.

P2 (Minor): The discussion section partially repairs this by explicitly stating “environment-scoped positioning rather than universal superiority” and that blocks are not pooled.
Phase 4 Technical Soundness
Channel and MAC modeling credibility vs claims

P1 (Major): The paper claims “explicit MAC-collision and multi-hop relay modeling” in the large-scale setting, but does not specify the MAC/interference model in sufficient detail to validate realism. The experimental setup lists only regime labels and “strict-physics flags.” Without (a) the interference model (protocol model vs SINR), (b) carrier-sense assumptions, (c) retransmission/capture modeling, and (d) traffic synchronization assumptions, “collision modeling” can easily become a simplistic Bernoulli drop process that does not reflect hidden-terminal behaviors.

P1 (Major): Your own motivation cites transitional-region behavior driven by path loss/shadowing and multipath fading—yet you do not declare the parameters that would determine whether such a transitional region exists in your simulator. Transitional-region work explicitly links PRR behavior to path loss exponent and shadowing variance. If you do not report those per environment (or provide measurement-based calibration), the “four environments” are essentially labels, not models.
CAS novelty vs prior art

P1 (Major): CAS appears to be an engineering integration / mode-selection policy rather than a clearly novel routing primitive, and the novelty claim is not defensible without positioning against recent “adaptive switching” routing work. A recent paper explicitly framed around “Adaptive Switching and Routing Protocol…” exists and should be discussed as near-neighbor prior art.

P1 (Major): CAS also resembles a family of hybrid cluster/chain schemes (LEACH↔PEGASIS style hybrids) published earlier; the manuscript should clarify what is fundamentally new (state variables? switching logic? stability guarantees? provable bounds?) rather than presenting “switching” itself as new. Even older hybrid protocols combine chain-based and cluster-based communication; the existence of such hybrids undermines broad novelty claims if not differentiated.
Gateway reinforcement and failure-mode reasoning

P1 (Major): “Gateway reinforcement” is under-specified in failure/attack scenarios and resembles existing gateway-based hierarchical routing lines. The literature contains explicit hierarchical gateway routing protocols. If AERIS gateway nodes fail, do you (a) re-elect gateways, (b) fall back to direct CH→BS, (c) avoid oscillations, and (d) quantify reliability degradation? None of this is convincingly addressed in the current text/experiments.
Energy model parameterization

P1 (Major): The manuscript does not report the numeric radio-energy parameters (e.g., E_elec, amplifier coefficients, threshold distance) that determine lifespan/energy tradeoffs in LEACH-family simulations. This is a reproducibility blocker: energy conclusions can flip if you choose different radio models or parameter sets. (I did not find explicit E_ELEC/E_amp values in the text.)
Phase 5 Presentation Quality
Figures, tables, and readability

The PDF is generally readable, but some figure/table design choices will create print-resolution risk.

P2 (Minor): Figures are mostly legible, but “dense heatmap + small numeric annotations” is borderline at print scale. In Figure 5, in-cell numeric effect sizes and ΔPDR values are small; some readers will struggle in print unless font size is increased or numeric overlays are moved to a supplementary table. (See rendered page above.)

P2 (Minor): Figure captions are relatively standalone and explicit about (n, CI bands, and which overlays are descriptive). For example, NS-3 figure caption clearly distinguishes primary vs descriptive overlays.
Table volume and necessity

P2 (Minor): 14 tables is heavy for a 20-page manuscript; several diagnostic tables should move to Supplementary Material. You already label one pilot table as diagnostic; that table belongs in supplement with a short mention in main text.
Length and writing quality

P2 (Minor): Length appears within a typical Sensors submission footprint (~20 pages), but the paper reads “process-heavy” due to many evidence blocks. The evidence fencing is good, but the narrative could be tightened by moving diagnostic artifacts to supplement and focusing the main paper on the primary matrix + a single validation.

P2 (Minor): Terminology is mostly standard, but some labels are idiosyncratic (e.g., “PDRexpected”) and should be aligned to community norms to reduce reviewer skepticism.
Overall Recommendation, Confidence, Strengths, Weaknesses

Overall Recommendation: Reject

This is not a “bad idea” paper; it’s a “trust and reproducibility” failure in its current form. Two key references are misattributed at the DOI level (fatal), and the protocol’s central empirical claim (“reliable under realistic channel conditions”) is not reproducible because the channel/MAC/energy parameterization is not specified at the level needed to replicate results.

Confidence Level: 5/5

The P0 findings are objective (DOI→paper mismatch), and the reproducibility gaps are directly grounded in what is and is not stated in the experimental setup and metric definition.

Top Strengths

    The paper attempts explicit evidence-regime fencing and avoids naive pooling across regimes (Table 2; discussion).
    Statistical intent is better than average for WSN routing submissions (Welch test + Holm + effect sizes stated).
    The paper acknowledges a major counterexample regime (PEGASIS dominating indoor_office at high scale) rather than claiming universal superiority.

Top Weaknesses

    P0: DOI-level misattribution of references 16 and 17 undermines the credibility of the related-work survey and the claim-trace chain.
    P1: Core simulation realism claims are not reproducible because channel/MAC (and key energy model parameters) are not specified beyond labels and “flags.”
    P1: The primary metric definition (PDRexpected) is non-standardly named and underspecified (“expected” denominator), creating risk of biased cross-protocol comparisons (especially vs TEEN-like designs).
